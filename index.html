How StepSignal works

One journey, end to end: a route becomes a chain of short stretches; each stretch is checked against real photographs and map data; what is found, and what is missing, is shown separately.

  1. 1 →

    Select a location and destination

    Search any city, address or landmark worldwide, or use your current location after explicit permission.

  2. 2 →

    Generate a walking route

    OSRM produces the route and, where available, an alternate for stretch-by-stretch comparison.

  3. 3 →

    Divide into ~50 m stretches

    Short stretches mean one hidden kerb cannot hide inside a long “accessible” segment.

  4. 4 →

    Retrieve photographs and map data

    Mapillary street imagery and OpenStreetMap features are gathered per stretch, with capture dates.

  5. 5 →

    Analyse with Meta Muse Spark

    Each real photograph returns structured observations with confidence. Unknown stays unknown.

  6. 6 →

    Present evidence and uncertainty

    Barriers, clear-looking stretches, and uncertain stretches are labelled and counted separately.

Why street-level evidence matters

A route can look perfect on a map and fail at a single kerb. Accessibility lives at street level: a stall across a footway, a footpath that simply ends, a flight of steps at a doorway. Those details rarely exist in map data, but they are visible in street photographs. StepSignal’s job is to surface them — with the photograph attached, so you can judge for yourself.

Why we show uncertainty

Most of the world’s pavements have never been photographed for this purpose, and map tags are patchy. An app that fills those gaps with a confident score is inventing reassurance. StepSignal instead treats “we don’t know” as first-class information: it is counted, mapped, and explained. That honesty is what makes the confirmed findings worth trusting.

How AI analyses real-world imagery

What the image shows

Muse Spark describes visible features only: steps, kerb ramps, tactile paving, obstructions, surface damage, crossings.

What might be an issue

Features are framed as potential barriers for a wheelchair user — an interpretation, labelled as such, not a certification.

How confident, what’s unknown

Every observation carries a confidence value, and anything the model cannot tell stays “unknown” on screen.

What needs a human

Old photographs, low-confidence readings, and unphotographed stretches are flagged for human verification — including yours, via the contribution form.

Help map the gaps

Six of the eighteen stretches in our verified example have no street photograph at all. Local knowledge closes gaps like those. Send a location or a Mapillary photo link and tell us what you noticed. Submissions are stored for review, analysed before they count as evidence, and are never displayed as verified on arrival. Prefer GitHub? Open an issue instead. Uploading street imagery to Mapillary under your own account also directly improves future coverage.

About StepSignal

StepSignal was built for the Meta Global AI Developer Hackathon. Data sources: Mapillary street imagery, OpenStreetMap and Overpass, OSRM walking routes, and Meta Muse Spark (`muse-spark-1.3`) for image analysis. Privacy: location is only requested when you ask for it on the map, no location history is stored or transmitted, and API keys never reach the browser. Limitations: photographic coverage and map tags vary worldwide and may be outdated — which is precisely why every claim in StepSignal carries its date and its evidence.

Frequently asked questions

Does StepSignal tell me a route is wheelchair accessible?

No — deliberately. A stretch is only ever described as having a confirmed barrier, having no barrier visible in the analysed evidence, or being uncertain. Photographs cannot certify a whole route.

What does “uncertain” mean?

We do not have enough analysable evidence for that stretch — no photograph, an unanalysed photograph, or too many unknown signals. Uncertain is never treated as accessible.

Where do photographs come from?

Real street-level images from Mapillary, sampled every ~50 m along the walking route. Every photograph keeps its Mapillary ID and capture date, shown wherever it is used. Imagery availability varies by location worldwide, and the app says so per route.

How does the AI work?

Each image is sent (as an image, not a URL) to Meta Muse Spark (muse-spark-1.3), which returns structured JSON: steps, kerb ramps, tactile paving, obstructions, discontinuities, crossings, crossing barriers and surface damage — each present, absent, or unknown, with confidence. Unknowns stay unknown.

Can the AI measure ramp gradients or widths?

No. Gradients are at most “visually suspected”, and widths and surface quality are never measured from a photograph.

Does StepSignal track my location?

Only if you choose it. Location is requested only when you tap “Use my current location” or enable live tracking on the map. Nothing is requested on page load, no location history is stored, and tracking can be stopped at any time.

How old is the evidence?

It varies per stretch, and the age is printed on every photograph. The verified Bandra example uses photos from 2021–2025.

I found a barrier that is not mapped. Can I add it?

Yes — use the form below. Paste a Mapillary link or describe the location. Submissions are queued for review and only become evidence after analysis.
